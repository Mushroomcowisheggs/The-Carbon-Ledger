# The Carbon Ledger

A data pipeline for carbon (US EPA GHGRP, facility-level + unit-level), forests
(Brazil INPE PRODES annual series + DETER alerts + BDQueimadas fire foci),
species (GBIF, three taxa), and national context (OWID / Global Carbon Budget,
World Bank). It produces a SQLite database, 46 indicator CSVs, 24 figures, and
an English-language analysis report.

## Quick start

```bash
python -m pip install --target vendor pyxlsb     # the only non-standard dependency (reads .xlsb)
python src/run_all.py --offline                  # reproduce from bundled data, no network
python src/run_all.py                            # full run, downloads only if a local file is missing
python src/run_all.py database                   # resume from a given stage
python src/run_all.py --list                     # list stage names
```


## Conventions and scope

- Grid 0.02° (~2.2 km); areas corrected by the cosine of latitude.
- Forest area calibrated to the official `area_km` attribute; annual increments
  use nested calibration (see analysis report §8.2).
- Annual series truncated at `DATA_VINTAGE_YEAR = 2025`; 2026 is a partial year
  and is explicitly excluded.
- Trends use Mann-Kendall + Theil-Sen; the bootstrap seed is fixed for
  reproducibility.
- Missing capabilities (Scope 2/3 tonnages, DETER for the Atlantic Forest, 30 m
  resolution) are listed item by item in section 8.3 of the report; no modelled
  values are substituted.

## Offline reproduction

The repository bundles every upstream payload under `data/raw` and the derived
tables under `data/interim`, so a full run needs no public data service:

```bash
python src/run_all.py --offline
```

`--offline` checks that the bundle is present, sets `CARBON_LEDGER_OFFLINE=1`,
and makes any stray request fail immediately instead of silently contacting
EPA, INPE, GBIF, OWID or the World Bank. Without the flag the pipeline still
prefers local files: each fetch stage reuses its bundled outputs and only
downloads when a file is missing.

Two derived artifacts are deliberately not bundled and are rebuilt locally
from the files above:

- `data/interim/fire_foci.csv` (442 MB, from `data/raw/fire/*.zip`)
- `db/environmental.db` (195 MB, from `data/interim/*.csv`)

Both exceed the 100 MB single-file limit of ordinary Git hosts. Every other
bundled file is under 50 MB; the bundle is 805 MB in total.

Licences and required attribution for all ten upstream sources are in
[`LICENSE`](LICENSE) and in the `sources` table of the database.

TWO LEDGERS, ONE PLANET: WHAT PUBLIC CLIMATE DATA ACTUALLY TELLS US

From American coal plants to Brazilian forest canopies, millions of public
records reveal how the world's carbon accounts are shifting, and where the
numbers stop adding up.

If you look only at what American power plants and factories burn on-site, the
United States appears to be making steady progress on climate change. In 2023,
the roughly 6,500 large industrial facilities regulated by the Environmental
Protection Agency reported 2,383 million tonnes of direct greenhouse-gas
emissions, down 9.3% from 2019 and a quarter lower than in 2010. Yet in the
very same year, to the very same agency, American fossil-fuel suppliers
reported 4,651 million tonnes of carbon embedded in the coal, oil, and gas they
sold into the economy. That figure was 5.4% higher than in 2019, and climbing.

Both numbers are accurate, and neither is a clerical error. Under US reporting
rules, emissions from smokestacks and emissions locked inside sold fuels are
tracked on separate books, by different industries, with no requirement to
reconcile them. Meanwhile, measured against a straightforward path from 2019 to
halving emissions by 2030 and reaching net zero by 2050, direct US industrial
emissions were still 233 million tonnes above target in 2023, barely two
million tonnes closer than the year before.

CLOSING PLANTS, NOT CLEANING THEM

Digging below facility totals into the underlying equipment reveals how
America's direct emissions actually fell. It was not primarily a story of
engineering upgrades making existing machinery cleaner. Between 2015 and 2023,
the amount of carbon emitted per unit of generating capacity at US coal plants
barely budged, remaining roughly 26 times higher than natural gas. What changed
was the fleet itself: utilities retired 43% of their coal-fired units,
shrinking the count from 1,118 to 641 and cutting coal emissions by 46%.
Natural gas stepped in to absorb much of the demand, with gas emissions rising
18% over the same period to 1,033 million tonnes.

Power generation accounted for more than three-quarters of the net decline
between 2019 and 2023, while oil and gas systems was the only major sector to
post a substantial increase, up 8.4%. Even the smooth national downward curve
masks a fierce tug-of-war on the ground. Among the 5,410 facilities that
reported in both 2015 and 2023, 1,554 slashed their emissions by at least a
quarter, removing 567 million tonnes, while 1,159 expanded by more than a
quarter, adding 179 million tonnes back. Coal-heavy states such as Illinois,
Indiana, Kentucky, Missouri, and Pennsylvania drove the biggest reductions,
though statistical resampling shows that trying to crown a single state as the
top reducer is mostly noise.

What separates the smokestack ledger from the fuel-supply ledger is not just
direction, but concentration. Direct emissions are scattered across 6,470
facilities, with the 20 largest accounting for less than a tenth of the total.
The supply ledger is nearly seven times more concentrated: just 632 companies
report into it, and the top 20 account for almost a third of all embedded
carbon. Corporate climate pledges mirror that divide. In the global Science
Based Targets registry, companies routinely promise roughly 50% cuts, but when
a pledge covers only their own operations and electricity, the average target
date is 2033. When it extends to the fuels and products moving through their
supply chains, the target slips a full decade, to 2043.

BRAZIL'S CARBON ACCOUNT IS WRITTEN IN TREES

Compare the United States with Brazil, and the mechanics of climate accounting
change completely. In 2024, the United States emitted 4,912 million tonnes of
fossil-fuel CO₂, or 14.2 tonnes per person. Brazil emitted 491 million tonnes
from fossil fuels, just 2.3 tonnes per person. Yet in 2023, Brazil's emissions
from land-use change reached 1,467 million tonnes, three times its fossil-fuel
total. While Brazil's fossil emissions shift by less than 70 million tonnes
over several years, its land-use emissions can swing by more than a billion
tonnes. In the United States, the main climate lever is the power grid; in
Brazil, it is standing forest.

Across Brazil's three major forest biomes—Amazonia, the Cerrado savanna, and
the Atlantic Forest—official satellite inventories from Brazil's space agency
(INPE) show steady loss over the past two decades, though each biome is
degrading in a distinct way. Between 2007 and 2025, Amazonia lost roughly
135,000 square kilometers of forest, averaging about 7,500 square kilometers a
year. That is only 4.1% of its massive baseline, yet the sheer scale is
enormous. The Cerrado, Brazil's agricultural frontier, has been hit far harder
in relative terms: it lost nearly a quarter of its forest cover between 2000
and 2025, over 326,000 square kilometers, at a median annual rate five times
higher than the Amazon's.

Total acreage, however, hides what is happening inside the canopy. As roads and
farms slice through woodland, deep interior habitat collapses much faster than
total area. In the Cerrado, the share of forest classified as intact interior
plunged from 37.8% in 2000 to just 6.0% in 2025, while the density of exposed
forest edges more than doubled. In the Atlantic Forest, which lost 15% of its
area over the same period, core habitat fell from 14.7% to less than 1%. The
number of distinct forest patches there actually ticked down slightly, not
because fragments reconnected, but because the smallest remnants disappeared
completely, leaving hollowed-out edges behind. Contrary to the common image of
deforestation marching steadily inward like a wall, only 10% to 15% of new
clearing borders the previous year's frontier. Instead, clearing punches holes
into the forest along road networks.

WHY ALERTS, FIRES, AND DEFORESTATION DON'T MOVE TOGETHER

Tracking those forests in real time is notoriously easy to misread. In 2024,
Brazil's rapid-response satellite alert system (DETER) logged more than 81,000
alerts in the Amazon, double the previous year's count. Yet when the official,
reviewed annual deforestation inventory (PRODES) came out, annual clearing in
the Amazon had actually fallen, from 7,809 to 6,069 square kilometers.

Both figures were valid because the two systems watch different things. PRODES
is an annual accounting of clear-cutting; DETER is an early-warning feed
sensitive to cloud cover, selective logging, and wildfires. Even inside DETER's
own records, burn scars cover two and a half times more ground than clear-cuts
(142,000 square kilometers versus 57,000) despite generating fewer than half as
many alerts. Treating alert counts as a direct proxy for deforested area is a
recipe for confusion. Where DETER excels is timing: its monthly data shows
clearing peaking in May in the Cerrado and September in the Amazon, timed a
full quarter ahead of the August-to-October dry season.

The same caution applies to fire. It is tempting to assume that the 5.1 million
fire detections recorded across Brazil between 2003 and 2024 are simply the
smoke rising from newly cleared forest. At the annual scale, the data refuses
to cooperate: across all three biomes, same-year fire counts and annual
deforestation rates do not move together in any statistically reliable way.
Only a one-year lag in the Atlantic Forest even hints at a link. Fire is
governed largely by drought and pasture management, whereas deforestation
responds to commodity prices and enforcement. Relative to remaining forest
area, it is actually the Cerrado, not the Amazon, that burns most intensely.
And because Brazil's reference fire-monitoring satellites have changed over the
years, year-to-year totals are best read for broad direction rather than exact
leaps.

WHERE PUBLIC DATA HITS A WALL

If carbon and forest records are tricky, linking deforestation to wildlife loss
with open data is harder still. After cleaning and deduplicating public
biodiversity records from GBIF for three well-studied groups in Brazil—bats,
palms, and primates—73,622 records remain. On the surface, species seem to
persist at very different rates across biomes and between deforested and intact
areas.

Look closer, though, and the pattern turns out to be a mirror of human effort.
Biologists have surveyed primates in the accessible Atlantic Forest ten times
more intensively than bats in the remote Amazon, averaging 86.5 records per
grid cell versus 8.6. Raw species persistence tracks how often scientists
visited, not how much forest survived. Once sampling effort is accounted for
statistically, the apparent 3-to-7-percentage-point drop in species retention
in deforested cells vanishes. What survives the test is narrower but still
meaningful: in the handful of locations where all three groups have been well
surveyed, the same local hotspots are losing bats, palms, and primates
together. With open occurrence data, the lesson is mostly methodological:
ignore where the collectors went, and your biodiversity map becomes a map of
field stations.

Stepping back, the real value of putting these ten public datasets side by side
is learning where the evidence is solid and where it isn't. Some conclusions
stand up to every cross-check: regulated US facilities are cutting emissions
faster than the wider US economy (falling from 56% of national fossil CO₂ in
2010 to 48% in 2023), coal retirements are doing the heavy lifting, US
fossil-fuel supply is still growing, and Brazil's forest interiors are
shrinking rapidly. Other questions simply cannot be answered from current
public filings. US facilities do not report purchased electricity or full
supply-chain tonnages; Brazil's rapid-alert system does not cover the Atlantic
Forest; and even standard ecological metrics like habitat connectivity can flip
from positive to negative depending on how far an animal is assumed to travel.

Good climate accounting rarely collapses into a single headline number. Reading
the ledgers honestly means keeping the accounts separate: what a country burns
versus what it sells, what a satellite flags in a month versus what an
inventory confirms in a year, and what the data proves versus where it falls
silent.
